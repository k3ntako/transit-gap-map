Map Stats Methodology

Methodology

How the map was built and what it leaves out. Figures on this page are read from the same build as the map; the full list is on the stats page.

What is being measured

The station set is the subway and the Staten Island Railway, plus the LIRR and Metro-North stations inside the city. Walking is routed on the OpenStreetMap pedestrian network, which means the East River, Newtown Creek, rail cuts and expressways all stop a walk the way they stop a person — the single thing a straight-line buffer gets wrong.

The under-service score

The floor is a floor and not a cap. Capping it was measured and fails: above a cap every area ties, and ties break on density alone, so the map turns into a population map with extra steps.

The score carries no jobs term, no equity term and no frequency term — not because those do not matter, but because each one was measured against the corridors agencies have proposed and each one lowered agreement. The equity variables ship anyway, weightable in the map's own slider, because the MTA defines its Equity Areas by exactly the zero-vehicle variable. The default weight is zero and the measurement is why.

Under-served zones

A rail line serves a strip of neighborhoods, so the unit that matters is not one area but a run of them. A zone is a group of neighboring areas that are all past the standard. Neighboring here means sharing a border on land: the census boundaries exclude water, so two areas facing each other across the East River do not touch and no bridge counts as a walk.

What the transit model includes

Buses are not in the score. That is an epistemic decision rather than a data one. Bus service is what the city provides instead of rail in precisely the places this map exists to find, so letting a bus close the gap would hide the finding by construction. The frequent-bus layer is drawn on top of the score instead, where it does the job of showing what runs today.

The Staten Island Ferry is in, and it is the same rule rather than an exception to it: the rule is about substitution, not about vehicles, and the ferry substitutes for nothing. It is the only fixed-guideway trunk link across the harbor, on its own right of way at a published headway, and no proposal replaces it. Without it, the Staten Island Railway would connect to nothing off the island, which overstates the gap rather than erring on the safe side.

Job access, measured as time

The general version is worth more than the specific fix: a threshold on the axis you are measuring along truncates, while a threshold on the axis you are measuring only rescales. Fixing the share of jobs and measuring the minutes is the second kind, which is why its exact value turns out not to matter — measured, the ranking holds at a Spearman correlation of 0.968 to 0.991 anywhere from 2% to 25%. The 45-minute figure stays published, because cumulative opportunity at a fixed budget is what peer analyses report, but it is no longer the number quoted about the worst-served places.

Commuter rail fares

Both framings are here because either one alone misleads. The citywide share hardly moves without the commuter railroads, since those areas were mostly outside the walk band anyway; the people living in them would walk far further each way.

Step-free access

Two cautions on the data behind it. The flag records that a step-free route was built, and elevators go out of service — MTA publishes live outages, and a map rebuilt once cannot say “out until April”. And some stations are step-free in one direction only, which a single yes-or-no per station flatters; the finer per-direction data is published and is not used here yet.

Limitations

  • A rider who would take either the local or the express still waits for whichever they board. Resolving that needs hyperpath routing rather than a shortest path. Fordham to Grand Central therefore reads 37 minutes against a real expected ~27, so the bias runs toward overstating under-service on commuter rail — the direction this project has to be most suspicious of.
  • Population is an estimate. ACS five-year figures carry margins of error that are wide for areas this small, and they are not shown on the map.
  • An area's median hides what happens inside it. The shortest and longest sample in each one are in the map's tooltip for that reason.
  • Jobs are workplaces, not commutes. They come from LEHD workplace counts, which say where jobs are, not which of them a given resident could hold or at what hour.
  • Stairs are a separate layer, not part of the score. In the score a station a wheelchair user cannot enter counts the same as one they can. The step-free layer above is the honest version of that question, and the reason it is kept apart is that a missing elevator is answered by an elevator rather than by a rail line.
  • Schedules, not service as it runs. Delays, crowding and gaps between the timetable and the day are outside this entirely, and so are PATH, NJ Transit, buses and bikes.
